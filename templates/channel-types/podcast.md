# Podcast / Interview Channel

> Long conversations that discover badly and monetize beautifully. The episode is the asset;
> the clip is the advertisement for it.

---

## 1. Channel DNA

Podcasts invert the usual economics: **discovery is structurally weak and monetization is
structurally strong**. A 30–90 minute conversation (`benchmarks.md` §3) generates enormous
watch time per view — 20+ minute videos already account for 57% of all platform watch time —
and clears the 8:00 mid-roll threshold many times over, so ad inventory is deep and sponsor
reads have somewhere to live. That is why brand deals, not AdSense, sit at the top of the
stack. The cost is that the format actively resists packaging: there is no demonstrable
outcome, no visual event, and one static two-shot to build a thumbnail from, so the same
episode that holds a viewer for 40 minutes cannot persuade a stranger to start it. The
consequence is not optional — **a paired clips channel (or a dedicated Shorts output) is the
discovery engine, and the long episode is the destination**, because on browse and suggested
surfaces a conversation competes against edited content and loses. Growth comes primarily from
**the guest's audience**, which means booking is a distribution decision, not an editorial one.
Audio-first cross-posting to podcast feeds runs in parallel with its own metrics that YouTube
Analytics never sees, so total reach is always understated inside the platform. The trap is
treating the video as a recording rather than a produced show — unstructured, unchaptered,
unedited, released because it happened.

## 2. Content Mix

| Type | Share | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Hub** (the recurring interview format) | 60–70% | The show itself. Same structure, same slot, different guest. |
| **Help** (topic episodes, solo or Q&A) | 15–25% | Searchable subject episodes that work without a name attached. |
| **Hero** (a guest well above the channel's tier) | 10–15% | Borrowed audience. 2–4 per year, planned around them. |

**Evergreen vs trending:** 50–60% evergreen. Conversations about ideas keep earning; episodes
pegged to a news cycle decay within weeks. Book at least half the calendar against subjects
that will still make sense in two years.

**The clips layer sits outside this mix.** Treat it as distribution, not content: 3–6 clips per
episode, each built around one self-contained claim, published where the audience scrolls
rather than where the episode lives.

## 3. Cadence & Length

| Setup | Long-form | Shorts | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solo | 1/week (4–5/month) | 5–7/week | Booking and post-production are the real constraint, not recording |
| Small team (2–3) | 2/week (8–10/month) | 8–12/week | Approaches the 12+/month growth threshold once clips count (`benchmarks.md` §4) |

**Optimal length:** 30–90 minutes (`benchmarks.md` §3). Runtimes beyond 90 minutes are common
in this format but sit outside any published benchmark band — treat 90+ as an editorial choice
you are making without data, not as a validated target.

**Mid-roll:** the 8:00 threshold (`benchmarks.md` §3, ~50% revenue increase) is irrelevant as a
target here — you clear it in the cold open. The real decision is mid-roll *density* and
placement: put breaks at topic transitions you can see in the chapter list, never mid-answer.

**Chapters are load-bearing for this archetype.** They are the only navigation a 60-minute
video has, and they convert a monolithic runtime into a menu a browsing viewer can enter
anywhere. Rules from `benchmarks.md` §6: must start at `0:00`, minimum 3, minimum 10 seconds
each; chapters correlate with **+4% AVD** (`B`). Write chapter titles as claims, not labels —
"Why the first hire is usually wrong" beats "Hiring".

## 4. Title Patterns

1. `[GUEST NAME] on [PROVOCATIVE CLAIM]`
2. `[GUEST NAME]: "[SHORT QUOTE FROM THE EPISODE]"`
3. `The [ROLE] Who [UNUSUAL ACHIEVEMENT] — [GUEST NAME]`
4. `Why [GUEST NAME] Thinks [CONTRARIAN POSITION]`
5. `[N] Hours With [GUEST NAME] on [SUBJECT]`
6. `[GUEST NAME] Explains What [INDUSTRY] Gets Wrong About [SUBJECT]`
7. `What [ROLE]s Never Say in Public — [GUEST NAME]`
8. `[SUBJECT], With Someone Who Actually [CREDENTIAL]`
9. `[GUEST NAME] Answers the [SUBJECT] Question Nobody Asks`
10. `Ep [NUMBER]: [GUEST NAME] — [HOOK PHRASE]`

**Length rule:** the dominant surfaces are suggested and external, both of which are read like
a feed rather than a results page, so keep titles at **50–65 characters** — shorter than a
search-led archetype, longer than a pure browse one (`benchmarks.md` §6, title-length
reconciliation). Whichever carries more pull — the guest's name or the claim — goes in the
**first 40–50 characters**, because that is all mobile shows. If the guest is unknown to your
audience, lead with the claim and put the name second; leading with an unrecognized name spends
the entire visible title on nothing. **Non-English channel:** treat the ten formulas as structures. How a guest's name and a claim are ordered, and which of the two carries the pull, differ by market — take the convention from local shows rather than translating the English (`references/localization-guide.md` §5).

## 5. Thumbnail Formula

- **Face:** required, and this is one of the few archetypes where two faces work — the guest's
  presence *is* the offer. Faces carry a **+20–30% CTR** lift (`benchmarks.md` §7). Crop tight;
  a wide studio shot wastes the frame on furniture.
- **Text:** ≤5 words, 3 ideal (`benchmarks.md` §7). Use the claim, never the guest's name — the face
  already says who it is. This is the Information Split Rule: thumbnail carries the emotional
  hook, title carries the name and the promise, and they must not repeat.
- **Colors:** 2–3, consistent across the whole series so the show is recognizable at thumbnail
  size before anything is read.
- **Composition:** exactly 1 focal point, 30–40% negative space. Guest larger than host if the
  guest is the draw; reverse if the show is the draw.
- **Avoid:** the raw studio two-shot with both people mid-sentence, the podcast logo, microphones
  as the visual subject, and screenshots of the recording. These are what "recording, not show"
  looks like from the outside.
- **Target CTR:** no podcast-specific figure is published. Diagnose against the traffic-source
  table (`benchmarks.md` §1): suggested traffic 9.5%, external 2.8%, browse 3.5%. A podcast
  pulling 3–4% blended is normal, not broken.

## 6. Hook Style

**Primary — Shock/Contradiction.** Cold-open on the single most disagreeable thing the guest
said, pulled from anywhere in the runtime, before any intro.
> "You said the entire industry has been measuring the wrong thing for a decade. Start there."

**Secondary — Social Proof.** Establish in one line why this specific person's answer is worth
60 minutes, using what they did rather than what they are called.
> "He's the person the other companies call when this breaks. He has never explained it publicly."

**Timing:** the cold open runs 15–45 seconds and must be a real excerpt, not a paraphrase.
Value proposition stated within 15 seconds buys **+18% retention at the 1-minute mark**, and
55% of viewers are lost in the first 60 seconds (`benchmarks.md` §2) — a podcast that opens
with pleasantries spends that budget on nothing. Pattern interrupts for pre-recorded long-form
run about every 30 seconds; in a static two-camera conversation the interrupt is a cut, a
b-roll insert, or an on-screen quote card, and if you have none of these your only interrupt is
the conversation itself changing direction.

Full taxonomy in `references/hook-library.md`.

## 7. Monetization Stack

> All figures are **US baseline**. Apply `references/localization-guide.md` before quoting
> revenue for a non-US channel.

| Rank | Stream | Why This Position |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Brand deals | The format's defining advantage: deep mid-roll inventory, a host read that sounds native, and a floor of $1,000+ per placement with multi-episode packages the norm (`benchmarks.md` §9) |
| 2 | AdSense | Enormous watch time per view and many ad slots per episode offset a mid-to-low RPM; 20+ minute videos already carry 57% of platform watch time (`benchmarks.md` §3) |
| 3 | Memberships | Ad-free feeds, bonus episodes and early access are the easiest membership offer of any archetype because the product already exists |
| 4 | External funnels | The audio feed, a newsletter and live events extend the same conversation off-platform; long-session listening builds unusual trust |
| 5 | Shopping affiliate | Works only where the sponsor category overlaps recommendable products; 5–20% commission (`benchmarks.md` §9) |
| 6 | Super Chat / Thanks | Near-zero on pre-recorded episodes; only live-recorded or premiered episodes see any |
| 7 | Shorts ad share | Structurally essential as the discovery engine and irrelevant as revenue — long-form RPM runs 10–100× Shorts RPM (`benchmarks.md` §9) |

**FTC disclosure is not optional and the platform toggle is not sufficient on its own**
(`benchmarks.md` §9): verbal disclosure within the first 15–30 seconds of the read, or on-screen
text held 10+ seconds, plus the first two lines of the description.

## 8. Growth Trajectory

| Tier | What Changes | Key Lever | Revenue Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–500 | Growth depends on borrowed audiences, so the tier goes to earning access to guests rather than to optimizing anything | Lock the format and the slot; book guests whose audience exceeds the show's | None — not yet monetized |
| 500–1K | Expanded YPP at 500 subscribers (`benchmarks.md` §9) opens audience-direct streams, and running clips and an audio feed in parallel starts compounding distribution | Launch the clips output and the audio feed in parallel — one recording, three surfaces | Audience-direct only, and immaterial at this size |
| 1K–10K | Full YPP at 1,000 subscribers (`benchmarks.md` §9); clips carry discovery while full episodes carry watch time, and the show becomes bookable | Clips for discovery, episode watch time for the algorithm and for the sponsor pitch | First ad revenue, and small — long episodes convert to ad revenue more slowly than tightly packaged formats |
| 10K–50K | Multi-episode sponsor packages become viable at the brand-deal floor (`benchmarks.md` §9) — the show sells a slate rather than a slot | Multi-episode sponsor packages sold on audience quality rather than raw reach | Meaningful but not replacement income, and markedly more predictable than ad-led revenue |
| 50K–100K+ | Sponsor inventory sells ahead of production, which is what makes this archetype's income forecastable at all | Inventory sold ahead; memberships and live events layered on top | Replacement-income territory, driven by sponsorship rather than by any RPM band |

Podcasts start slower than almost any archetype and plateau later, because early growth depends on borrowed audiences the
show must earn access to. The compensating property: once a show has a sponsor list, its income is far more predictable
than an ad-led channel of comparable size.

> ⚠️ Tier progression is not a timeline. `benchmarks.md` §11 lists growth-timeline and revenue-by-tier as known gaps — no verified data exists. Any revenue figure must be modeled from the creator's own audience size and geography via `references/localization-guide.md`, never read off a table.

## 9. Failure Modes

**The recording released as a show.** Two cameras, no cold open, no chapters, no cuts, published
because the session happened. Retention is fine for the people who already listen and the video
is invisible to everyone else, so the channel's ceiling is its existing audience.
**Fix:** impose a fixed production spec before the next episode — 15–45s cold open cut from the
best moment, minimum 6 chapters written as claims, three mid-roll breaks placed at topic
transitions, and one on-screen quote card per 10 minutes. No episode publishes without all four.

**No clips layer, so nothing discovers.** All effort goes into the episode, the episode only
reaches subscribers, and the creator concludes the format doesn't grow. It does — the long
video was simply never the discovery unit.
**Fix:** commit to 3–6 clips per episode as a non-negotiable part of the deliverable, each built
around one complete claim with a hard 1–3 second hook window (`benchmarks.md` §8), and put them
on a paired clips channel or dedicated Shorts feed rather than mixing them into the main upload
stream.

**Booking as the entire strategy.** Every episode depends on who is in the chair, so an
unrecognizable guest means a dead week and there is no reason to watch the show for its own
sake. The channel is a funnel for other people's audiences with nothing retaining them.
**Fix:** build one recurring segment that belongs to the show and not the guest — a fixed
closing question, a standing scoreboard, a segment format viewers can anticipate — and put it in
every episode for the next ten, then check whether returning-viewer rate has separated from
guest reach.
