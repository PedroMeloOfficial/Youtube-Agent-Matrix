# Retention & Scripting Guide

**Purpose.** How to build a script that holds attention by construction — anatomy, beat sheets, loop mechanics, pacing math and a repair procedure.

**Hook *types* live in `hook-library.md`. Numbers live in `benchmarks.md`** — retention and interrupt data are §2, length bands and spoken-word pacing are §3, disclosure requirements are §9.

**Contents:** 1 Anatomy of a retention-engineered script · 2 Beat-sheet skeletons · 3 Open loops and transitions · 4 Pattern interrupts and b-roll planning · 5 Energy patterns · 6 Pacing, word-count math and line craft · 7 CTAs, disclosures and warnings · 8 Diagnosing and repairing a script from a retention curve · 9 Script self-check · 10 Decision rules

## 1. Anatomy of a retention-engineered script

A script is four layers running at once. Amateur scripts write only the first; retention comes from the other three.

| Layer | What it is | Failure symptom |
|---|---|---|
| **Content** | The information, argument or story | Nothing to say; the video is padding |
| **Tension** | Open loops, unanswered questions, unresolved stakes | Steady decline with no reason to stay |
| **Rhythm** | Interrupts, energy shifts, sentence-length variation | Flat curve that slowly bleeds — "boring but correct" |
| **Promise** | The contract set by title and thumbnail, and its repayment | Early cliff; metadata mismatch (`algorithm-guide.md` §9) |

**Deliver the promise immediately.** The title and thumbnail are a contract. The opening must *visibly begin paying it* — not preview it, not contextualize it. State the value proposition inside the window in `benchmarks.md` §2 and begin the payoff before the viewer has to decide whether to trust you. **The sequence is pay, then escalate:** give what was promised, then reveal there is more. Withholding the promised thing to "build anticipation" spends retention you have not earned.

| Segment | Share of runtime | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | First 30 seconds | Stop the exit, state the promise, open a loop (`hook-library.md`) |
| Setup | ~10% | Frame stakes and scope — the shortest section that can do the job |
| Body | ~70% | Beats, each ending by opening the next |
| Payoff | ~15% | The thing the video was for — never the last 10 seconds |
| Outro | Final seconds | Session continuation: end screen, next video |

**The payoff must not be the final beat** — land it while the viewer is still watching, then give them somewhere to go.

## 2. Beat-sheet skeletons

Four reusable structures. Percentages are of runtime, so they scale to any band in `benchmarks.md` §3. `L#` opens a loop, `L#✓` pays it off.

### 2.1 Tutorial / how-to — intent: solve a problem now
*Failure mode: front-loading prerequisites. Every second before the first visible progress is paid for in retention.*

| # | Beat | % | Content | Loops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Result-first hook + scope | 0–10 | Show the finished outcome, then state exactly what they'll be able to do and how long it takes | L1: "the step most people skip" |
| 2 | Step 1 | 10–28 | The easiest visible win, delivered fast; prerequisites in one line or in the description | L2: a mistake teased |
| 3 | Step 2 | 28–46 | Core work | L2✓ |
| 4 | Step 3 — the differentiator | 46–66 | The non-obvious step that justifies the video existing | L1✓ |
| 5 | Common failure mode | 66–80 | What goes wrong and how to fix it — high save value | L3: "one more thing" |
| 6 | Result + verification | 80–92 | How they know it worked | L3✓ |
| 7 | Next step | 92–100 | The natural follow-up video | — |

### 2.2 Video essay / explainer — intent: understand something
*Failure mode: answering the central question up front. State the thesis early; withhold the proof, not the direction.*

| # | Beat | % | Content | Loops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Provocation + thesis | 0–10 | The claim or anomaly that shouldn't be true, then plainly where this is going | L1: the central question |
| 2 | Why the obvious answer is wrong | 10–22 | Dismantle the assumption the viewer arrived with | L2: "the real reason" |
| 3 | Evidence block A | 22–38 | First pillar, with its own mini-payoff | — |
| 4 | Complication | 38–50 | The fact that breaks pillar A | L3 |
| 5 | Evidence block B | 50–66 | Resolves the complication, deepens the thesis | L2✓ |
| 6 | Strongest counterargument | 66–80 | Steelman it, then answer it — credibility is won here | L3✓ |
| 7 | Synthesis + implication | 80–100 | Thesis restated and now earned; open the next question | L1✓, L4 → next video |

### 2.3 Review / comparison — intent: decide
*Failure mode: saving the verdict for the end. Deliver it early, spend the video on why (`algorithm-guide.md` §9).*

| # | Beat | % | Content | Loops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Verdict-adjacent hook | 0–5 | The single most surprising finding | L1: "but there's a catch" |
| 2 | Who this is and isn't for | 5–10 | Qualify the audience immediately | — |
| 3 | Headline verdict | 10–16 | Say it — withholding costs more than it gains | L2: "and the reason surprised me" |
| 4 | Criterion 1, strongest | 16–34 | Demonstrate, don't describe | — |
| 5 | Criterion 2 | 34–50 | Where it beats the alternatives | — |
| 6 | The catch | 50–64 | The real weakness — credibility beat | L1✓ |
| 7 | Head-to-head | 64–80 | Direct comparison against the obvious alternative | L2✓ |
| 8 | Segmented recommendation | 80–100 | Who should adopt, at what price, plus one named alternative | — |

### 2.4 Narrative / story — intent: find out what happened
*Failure mode: chronological order. Story time and screen time differ — a cold open at the peak buys the first 30 seconds.*

| # | Beat | % | Content | Loops |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cold open at the peak | 0–5 | Drop into the most charged moment, out of order | L1: how did we get here |
| 2 | Reset and stakes | 5–14 | Who, what's at risk, why it matters | L2: the outcome teased |
| 3 | Inciting event | 14–26 | The thing that started it | — |
| 4 | Escalation | 26–40 | First complication; raise the cost | L3 |
| 5 | False resolution | 40–52 | It looks solved — where curves dip, so make it the most vivid beat | — |
| 6 | Reversal | 52–66 | It wasn't solved. Bigger problem | L3✓ |
| 7 | Lowest point → resolution | 66–88 | Maximum stakes, then we reach the cold-open moment | L1✓ L2✓ |
| 8 | Aftermath / meaning | 88–100 | Consequence and takeaway | — |

## 3. Open loops and transitions

### Loop mechanics

An open loop is an explicit unanswered question the viewer wants closed. Loops produce the suspension-bridge curve in `benchmarks.md` §2.

| Stage | How | Common error |
|---|---|---|
| **Open** | State the question concretely and say *when* it gets answered ("by step three") | Vague teasing — "stick around, it gets crazy" opens nothing |
| **Sustain** | Reference it once or twice mid-video without answering | Silence; the viewer forgets the loop existed |
| **Pay off** | Answer explicitly and mark it ("here's the thing I promised") | Answering implicitly, so the viewer never registers payment |

**Nesting.** Run 2–4 concurrent loops on different spans: a **macro** loop across the whole video (usually the title's question), a **mid** loop across one section, and **micro** loops of 30–90 seconds. Stagger openings and payoffs so a payoff always lands while another loop is still open. **A moment where no loop is open is a scripted exit point** — the viewer has closure and no reason to stay.

**The cost of an unpaid loop** is worse than never opening it: the viewer registers a broken promise, which suppresses satisfaction signals and trust in the next video. Track every loop opened and confirm every one is paid before the outro. If a loop cannot be paid, delete its opening. Open the first loop inside the hook and never let a section pass without opening a new one.

### Transitions

A transition is where retention leaks — four types, each with a distinct function:

| Type | Function | Template | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Escalation** | Next beat is bigger | "If that surprised you, [NEXT] is worse." | "That was the cheap version. The expensive one fails faster." |
| **Reversal** | Next beat contradicts this one | "Everything I just showed works — until [CONDITION]." | "That's the standard method. It also breaks the moment you scale it." |
| **Consequence** | Next beat is caused by this one | "Which creates a problem: [PROBLEM]." | "Which means every result after this point is wrong." |
| **Direct address** | Re-establish the viewer's presence | "You're thinking [OBJECTION]. You're right, and here's why." | "You're going to want to skip this step. Don't." |

**Dead transitions to eliminate:** "so," "anyway," "moving on," "next up," "another thing," "as I said." Each announces a boundary without giving a reason to cross it. **The transition test:** cover the next beat and read the transition line alone — if it does not make you want to see what follows, it is a label, not a transition.

## 4. Pattern interrupts and b-roll planning

An interrupt is any change that resets habituation. Frequencies per format, and the measured effects of early and drop-off-targeted interrupts, are in `benchmarks.md` §2.

| Type | Examples | Best used |
|---|---|---|
| **Visual** | Angle change, cut to b-roll, punch-in, location change | The default interrupt, everywhere |
| **Graphic** | Text pop, lower third, diagram, highlight box | Dense information beats |
| **Audio** | Music shift, sound effect, silence, tempo change | Section boundaries; before a key line |
| **Structural** | Format shift — talking head → screen share → demo | Every major beat |
| **Content** | Unexpected fact, question to camera, a contradiction | Anywhere the script goes flat — free to produce |
| **Pace** | A sudden very short sentence after long ones | Immediately before a payoff |

**Placement is the leverage; frequency is only a floor.** Place interrupts at known drop-off points from the last same-format video's curve; *before* a necessary low-stimulation segment, not after the viewer has gone; at every beat transition; and inside the first seconds as part of the hook. **Anti-pattern:** evenly spaced interrupts become their own pattern and stop interrupting.

**B-roll is scripted, not decided in post.** Every script row carries `TIME · VO · VISUAL · SOURCE · INTERRUPT-TYPE`. Anything that can be shown is shown rather than narrated — narrating a demonstrable claim wastes both the word budget and the interrupt. No visual holds longer than the format's interrupt interval. Flag every unsourceable visual before the script is approved; a beat depending on footage that does not exist gets cut or padded at edit time.

## 5. Energy patterns

Energy is the deliberate shape of delivery intensity. Pick one per video and *write* to it — energy is scripted, not only performed.

| Pattern | Shape | Best for | How to write it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gradual Slowdown** | Opens at maximum, decreases steadily | Short videos, single-impact pieces | Front-load the shortest sentences and highest-stakes claims; let clauses lengthen |
| **Calm-Burst Oscillation** | Calm stretches punctuated by regular bursts | Tutorials, educational | Calm instructional passages, then a burst line at each step boundary |
| **Anchor Pattern** | Returns to the core thesis at intervals | Video essays, long explainers | Write one anchor sentence; place a variation of it at each section end |
| **Strategic Pauses** | Silence and slowdown before key moments | Storytelling, dramatic reveals | Mark `[pause]` before every reveal; shorten the sentence that follows |
| **Progressive Energy** | High opening, stabilize, then variety | Vlogs, entertainment, long-form | Highest-energy material first, then alternate formats to sustain novelty |

**Match the pattern to viewer intent, not creator preference:** problem-solving wants Calm-Burst; understanding wants Anchor; entertainment wants Progressive; narrative wants Strategic Pauses. **Never run flat energy** — a monotone script fails even when the content is correct, and it is the most common cause of a steady-decline curve with no identifiable drop point.

## 6. Pacing, word-count math and line craft

Every length decision derives from **the speaking rate for `OUTPUT LANGUAGE`**, written `R` below. Get `R` from `localization-guide.md` §7, which also carries the 200-word timing test that produces the creator's own measured rate — always prefer that measured rate to any table. **`~140 words/minute` is the English baseline** (`benchmarks.md` §3) and is not transferable: syllable-timed and mora-timed languages differ substantially from it, and for German, Japanese, Chinese and Thai the right unit is not words at all.

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Script word count | `target minutes × R` |
| Runtime from a draft | `word count ÷ R` |
| Words per beat | `beat % × total word count` |
| Words available in a hook window | `seconds ÷ 60 × R` |

**Worked example — English, so `R = 140`.** A 12-minute target → `12 × 140 = 1,680` words. In the tutorial skeleton, beat 4 (46–66% = 20% of runtime) gets `0.20 × 1,680 ≈ 336` words. The 30-second hook gets `0.5 × 140 = 70` words — the entire budget for stopping the exit, stating the promise and opening a loop. **The arithmetic is the same in every language; only `R` changes.** For a language counted in characters rather than words, substitute characters for words throughout and read `R` in characters/minute.

**Adjust the rate down** for dense technical or numeric content (comprehension needs headroom), for narrative and demonstration (visuals carry time without words), and for any script with heavy b-roll. **Adjust up** only for high-energy entertainment. **Vary sentence length deliberately** — long, long, short; the shortest sentence in a section belongs immediately before that section's most important line. Choose the runtime band from `benchmarks.md` §3 *before* writing, then write to the word count; never pad toward a length threshold, because retention lost to padding costs more than the threshold gains.

**Write against AI cadence.** Synthetic and AI-styled narration underperforms substantially on retention (`benchmarks.md` §2), and much of the effect is sentence construction — a script written by an agent inherits it even when a human reads it.

| AI tell | Write this instead |
|---|---|
| Uniform sentence length | Vary deliberately: long, long, short |
| Hedging ("it's important to note", "generally speaking") | State the claim, then qualify in a separate short sentence |
| No first-person stake or opinion | Take a position the viewer can react to |

**The read-aloud test:** read it out loud at speaking pace and rewrite any sentence you stumble on or would not say to a person. **Specificity beats fluency** — one concrete number, name or moment beats a paragraph of well-formed generality.

## 7. CTAs, disclosures and warnings

The end of a video is reached by a small fraction of viewers, so a CTA placed only at the end is seen by almost nobody.

| Position | Reaches | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| After the first delivered payoff | The largest share of the audience | Subscribe or like — one sentence, no momentum break |
| Mid-video, after a second payoff | Smaller, higher intent | The commercial ask: product, newsletter, sponsor |
| End screen | Smallest, highest intent | Next video or playlist — session continuation |

**The dual-CTA question.** Two CTAs outperform one because they reach different audience slices; three or more read as begging and cost retention. **Use two**, place the first only after the viewer has received something, and make the asks different — two identical subscribe prompts is one CTA repeated. Verbal *and* on-screen together outperforms verbal alone. Never open a CTA with an apology; it flags an interruption the viewer might have ignored.

| Situation | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Distressing subject matter | Warning in the first 15 seconds, in the description's visible window, and as a chapter marker |
| Medical, legal or financial content | Disclaimer early and in the description — never inside the hook, where it costs retention |
| Sponsored content | Disclosure timing and format are compliance requirements — see `benchmarks.md` §9 |

**Placement principle:** a warning belongs *after* the hook and *before* the material it warns about — in the first five seconds it trades away the hook, any later it defeats its purpose.

## 8. Diagnosing and repairing a script from a retention curve

Read the curve first (`analytics-guide.md` §6), then map each timestamp back to the script.

| Curve feature | Script diagnosis | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Cliff in the first seconds | Hook did not pay the title's promise | Rewrite the hook against `hook-library.md`, or retitle instead |
| Drop at ~30s | Promise clarified and it wasn't what they clicked for | Align the value proposition with the packaging, or repackage |
| Steep slope, no single drop point | Flat energy, thin interrupt density | Choose an energy pattern (§5); add interrupts at beat boundaries |
| Sharp drop at a specific beat | A digression, or a dead transition into it | Cut the beat, or replace its entry transition (§3) |
| Valley then recovery | Slow segment the audience waited out | Shorten it, or move it earlier where tolerance is higher |
| Long flat stretch then a cliff | A loop closed with nothing opened after it (§3) | Open the next loop before closing the previous one |
| Drop right before the payoff | The payoff arrives too late for the remaining audience | Move it earlier; add a mid-video mini-payoff |
| Spike | Something worked | Identify it and build the next script around more of it |

**Repair order:** hook → promise alignment → loop structure → interrupt placement → energy → line-level pacing. Fixing pacing on a script whose hook is broken changes nothing measurable. **Repair one layer per iteration** and re-measure on the next video of the same format; rewriting everything at once makes the lesson unattributable.

## 9. Script self-check

Run against any script before delivery. Any `No` is a rewrite, not a note.

| # | Check |
|---|---|
| 1 | Does the first line work with no greeting, channel name, or "in this video"? |
| 2 | Is the title's promise visibly being paid before the value-proposition window in `benchmarks.md` §2 closes? |
| 3 | Is a loop open before the hook ends, is every loop explicitly paid off, and is there any later point where **no** loop is open? |
| 4 | Does every beat transition use escalation, reversal, consequence or direct address, with zero dead transitions (§3)? |
| 5 | Does interrupt spacing meet the format's frequency in `benchmarks.md` §2 — and is the spacing uneven? |
| 6 | Is one named energy pattern chosen, and visible in the writing? |
| 7 | Does word count match `target minutes × R`, ±10% — `R` being the speaking rate for `OUTPUT LANGUAGE` (`localization-guide.md` §7; ~140 wpm is the English baseline) — with runtime inside the band in `benchmarks.md` §3 and no padding? |
| 8 | Does every beat have a planned `VISUAL` and a checked `SOURCE`, and exactly two CTAs after a delivered payoff with different asks? |
| 9 | Does the payoff land before the outro, and does the ending point at a next video or playlist? |
| 10 | Does it pass the read-aloud test (no AI tells, §6), carry a concrete specific per section, and place any required warning per §7? |

## 10. Decision rules

- **If** writing any script → pick the format's beat sheet (§2) and the runtime band from `benchmarks.md` §3 *before* writing a line.
- **If** the format is a tutorial or review → deliver the outcome or verdict early and spend the video on *why*. Withholding it is a metadata mismatch (`algorithm-guide.md` §9).
- **If** a loop is opened → it must be paid off explicitly, or the opening is deleted. No exceptions.
- **If** at any point no loop is open → insert one. That point is a scripted exit.
- **If** the curve shows an early cliff → rebuild the hook (`hook-library.md`) or retitle. Do not touch the body.
- **If** the creator wants to pad toward the mid-roll threshold (`benchmarks.md` §3) → refuse, and propose adding a real beat instead.
- **If** repairing a script → fix one layer, re-measure on the next same-format video, and never rewrite all layers at once.
- **If** the curve shows a steady steep slope with no drop point → the problem is energy and interrupt density, not content.
- **If** a beat has no planned visual → make it talking head with a compensating interrupt, or cut it.
- **If** word count does not match `minutes × R` ±10% → the runtime estimate is wrong, not the pacing.
- **If** the script is not in English → read `R` from `localization-guide.md` §7 before any length math, and never carry the ~140 wpm English baseline across languages.
- **If** only one CTA is planned → add a second with a different ask. **If** three or more → cut to two.
- **If** narration will be synthetic → state the retention cost from `benchmarks.md` §2 explicitly and apply every §6 rewrite before delivery.
- **If** the opening contains a greeting, a channel name, or "in this video" → rewrite it (`hook-library.md`).
- **If** any self-check item in §9 returns `No` → the script is not deliverable.
