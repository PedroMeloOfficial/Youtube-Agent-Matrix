# Community Guide

**Purpose.** Everything that happens between uploads: the Community tab, comments, building a returning audience, live and premieres, collaborations, mining comments for ideas, surviving a viral moment or a pile-on, and staying in the job long enough for any of it to compound.

**Numbers live in `benchmarks.md`** — the comment-reply reach effect is §5, end-screen CTR is §6, cadence effects are §4. Note that §11 lists community-tab, live and premiere performance benchmarks as **known gaps**: when asked for one, say it is unavailable rather than inventing it.

## 1. The Community tab

The tab is the only surface where a channel reaches its subscribers without making a video. It is not a broadcast channel; it is a low-cost way to keep a relationship warm between uploads.

| Post type | Effort | Typical reach behaviour | Best used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Poll | Very low | Highest engagement per unit of effort — voting is one tap | Topic validation, thumbnail choice, format questions |
| Image + caption | Low | Strong; images stop the scroll | Announcing an upload, behind-the-scenes, a result |
| Text only | Low | Weakest of the types | Short updates, questions, apologies |
| Video / Short embed | Low | Good, and it pushes the video | Resurfacing an old video that still holds up |
| Quiz | Low | High engagement; the answer reveal drives a second look | Educational channels, testing a misconception |

**What actually gets reach:** posts that ask for a one-tap response, posts with a face or a strong image, posts that arrive when the audience is already active (aligned to the channel's usual upload time), and posts that do not simply say "new video is up" without giving a reason to care.

**Cadence.** 2–4 posts a week is a reasonable ceiling for most channels; more than daily trains subscribers to scroll past. Below one a week, the tab does nothing. The one non-negotiable post is the publish-day announcement — it is the most reliable way to reach subscribers who did not get a notification.

**What not to post:** anything asking for an action the viewer cannot take in one tap; recycled promo with no new framing; a poll whose result you have already decided to ignore; and negativity about the channel's own performance, which reads as a request for reassurance and costs authority.

## 2. Polls as validation — before producing

The highest-value use of the tab. A poll is the cheapest audience research available, and it runs before any production cost is sunk.

| Question to answer | Poll design | Read the result as |
|---|---|---|
| Which topic next? | 3–4 concrete video titles, not themes | A demand ranking — but weight it against search demand, not instead of it |
| Which angle? | Two framings of the same topic | Which promise the audience finds more interesting |
| Which thumbnail concept? | 2–3 described concepts, or images | Directional only; native A/B testing (`benchmarks.md` §7) is the real test |
| Is this a real problem? | "Have you ever hit X? yes / no / sort of" | Whether a product or deep-dive has demand (`monetization-guide.md` §10) |
| Format preference | "Long deep-dive or short practical?" | A cadence and length signal |

**Reading polls honestly.** A poll samples the most engaged subscribers — the people most likely to watch anything. It tells you what your core audience wants; it says nothing about what a stranger on the home feed would click. Use polls to choose *between* validated options, never to decide whether a topic has an audience at all. And never run a poll whose losing option you were going to make anyway; visibly ignoring a poll costs more than the poll was worth.

## 3. Comment strategy

**The reach effect.** Replying to a large share of comments quickly after publication correlates with materially higher reach (`benchmarks.md` §5). The mechanism is plausible: replies generate more comments, comment activity is a satisfaction signal, and early signals govern whether the video advances through the testing cascade. Treat the first two hours after publication as a work block, not as downtime.

**A practical publish-day routine:**
1. Publish, then pin the seeded comment (below) immediately.
2. Reply to every comment for the first 60–120 minutes. Short replies count.
3. Heart generously — it is one tap and it is visible to the commenter.
4. After the window, reply to questions and substantive comments only.
5. Return once at 24 hours and once at 72 hours for a shorter pass.

**What to pin, and why.** Pin exactly one comment, from the channel, and choose it deliberately:

| Pin choice | Use when |
|---|---|
| A discussion prompt | The default. It converts passive viewers into commenters and gives the section a shape. |
| A correction | Something in the video was wrong. Correct it loudly; it builds more trust than being right did. |
| Missing context or a resource | Links, timestamps, sources, the tools page (`monetization-guide.md` §9). |
| The best viewer comment | Rewards the audience and signals what kind of comments you want. |
| A disclosure | Required for sponsored content — but pinning is a supplement, never the whole disclosure. |

**Seeding a discussion from the video's CTA.** The comment section is shaped by what the script asks for. "Let me know what you think" produces nothing. A specific, answerable, low-effort question produces a section. Put the question in the script, near a moment of high engagement rather than at the very end, and repeat it as the pinned comment so the two match. Best-performing shapes: a binary choice ("which would you have done?"), an experience prompt ("what's the worst one you've seen?"), and a gap prompt ("what did I miss?") — which doubles as free research (§8).

**Moderation settings** — configure once, at channel level:

| Setting | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Held for review | On for potentially inappropriate comments; review in batches |
| Blocked words | Add slurs, the creator's known harassment terms, and spam patterns (crypto, "telegram", contact bait) |
| Link handling | Hold comments with links by default; scam links are the most common attack |
| Moderators | Appoint trusted regulars once volume exceeds what the creator can read |
| Comment defaults | Set at channel level so no upload is ever accidentally left open with no filters |

## 4. Hostility, and when to disable comments

Distinguish three things, because they need different responses:

| Type | Looks like | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Criticism | Substantive, about the work | Engage. Publicly agreeing with a fair criticism is one of the cheapest trust-building moves available. |
| Trolling | Provocation seeking a reaction | Do not reply. Hide the comment (the author still sees it; nobody else does). Never argue. |
| Harassment | Targeted, repeated, personal | Block, report, document. Do not engage at all. |

**Hide, don't delete** for borderline cases — hiding avoids the "you deleted my comment" escalation. Never reply angrily; a screenshot of a creator's angry reply outlives the comment by years.

**Disabling comments** is a legitimate tool, not a defeat. Turn them off when: the video concerns a topic that reliably attracts harassment; a pile-on is underway and moderation cannot keep up (§9); the subject involves a private individual or a minor; or the creator's own wellbeing requires it. Accept the cost — comments are an engagement signal and a research source — and prefer *restricting* (hold everything for review) over disabling where possible. Turning them back on later is fine and requires no explanation.

## 5. Building a returning audience

**Why returning viewers are the compounding metric.** New viewers cost impressions to acquire and are re-acquired from zero every video. A returning viewer arrives with intent, clicks at a higher rate, watches longer, and gives the video the early signal it needs to advance through the testing cascade (`benchmarks.md` §5). Subscriber count is a vanity number; returning-viewer share is the number that predicts whether the channel is actually building something. Find it in Analytics under the audience tab, alongside "unique viewers" and "returning viewers."

| Instrument | How it earns a return | Implementation |
|---|---|---|
| End screens | Sends a finishing viewer straight into another video | Last 15–20s; one video + one playlist beats four elements (`benchmarks.md` §6) |
| Playlists | Turns one view into a session | `repurposing-guide.md` §4 |
| Series | A promise to come back | Number the episodes and finish the run |
| Upload-schedule signalling | Makes returning a habit, not a decision | Say the day out loud; put it in the banner and the About section |
| A recurring segment | A reason to open every video | The same feature in the same place every time |
| Community posts | Keeps the channel present between uploads | §1 |
| Naming the next video | Converts interest into anticipation | The last spoken line of the video |

**On cadence promises.** Only announce a schedule the creator can hold in a bad month, not a good one. A missed weekly promise costs more than never having made one — it teaches viewers that the channel's commitments are unreliable, which is exactly the belief that stops them returning. Under-promise, then over-deliver by publishing extra. See §10.

## 6. Live streaming and premieres

**Fit by archetype:**

| Archetype | Live | Premiere | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaming | Excellent | Weak | Live *is* the format; tips are a primary stream |
| Talk / commentary / reaction | Strong | Strong | Personality is the product |
| Educational / tutorial | Q&A and office hours only | Good | Teaching live is inefficient; premieres suit a course drop |
| Documentary / high-production | Weak | Excellent | The work is the polish; a premiere creates an event around it |
| Faceless / compilation | Poor | Poor | Nothing to be present for |
| Vlog / lifestyle | Situational | Good | Depends entirely on parasocial strength |

**Live requires an audience that already exists.** Streaming to nobody is demoralizing and produces a bad VOD. Below a reliable concurrent audience, use scheduled Q&As announced days in advance rather than spontaneous streams.

**Structuring a premiere:**
1. Schedule 24–72 hours out — long enough to set reminders, short enough to stay urgent.
2. Announce via community post plus the end of the previous video.
3. Use the countdown; write a trailer if the platform offers one.
4. **Be present in chat for the whole runtime.** A premiere with an absent creator is just a video with a worse start.
5. Pin the discussion prompt as chat opens.
6. Afterwards it becomes an ordinary VOD — the premiere chat replay stays, which is a mild positive.

**Chat moderation for live:** appoint moderators before going live, never during. Set slow mode by default. Enable the profanity filter and members-only mode if a pile-on is possible. Agree a rule with moderators in advance about what gets timed out versus banned, so nobody improvises under pressure.

**Converting live to VOD:** a raw multi-hour stream is a poor VOD. Trim the dead opening, add chapters at topic changes, replace the thumbnail (the auto-grab is always bad), rewrite the title away from "LIVE — 14 Mar", and cut the highlights into Shorts (`repurposing-guide.md` §6). A stream is source material, not a finished upload.

## 7. Collaborations

**Finding a fit.** Look for *adjacent, not identical* audiences: enough overlap that the recommendation makes sense, enough difference that each side gains people they would not otherwise reach. Similar size matters more than most creators admit — a large mismatch turns a collaboration into unpaid promotion for the smaller party, or a favour the larger one resents.

| Check before proposing | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Audience adjacency | Their audience would plausibly subscribe to you |
| Size proximity | Within roughly a 3× band of each other |
| Values alignment | You would defend their back catalogue if asked |
| Format compatibility | One shared format works for both channels |
| Actual audience overlap | Not so high that everyone has already seen both |

**What a good collaboration trades.** Not "exposure" — audience *trust transfer*. The other creator's endorsement is what moves, and it only moves if the collaboration is genuinely worth watching. Both sides should bring something the other lacks: an expertise, an access, a location, a perspective, a skill.

| Formats that work | Formats that do not |
|---|---|
| Genuine conversation between two people with different views | Generic "we made a video together" with no premise |
| One teaches, one learns (visibly) | Both talking about how great the other is |
| A challenge or constraint that neither could do alone | A silent cameo with no context |
| Complementary expertise on one problem | Shoutout swaps — no content, no trust transfer |
| A shared series across two channels | Collaborations arranged by size rather than by fit |

**Practicalities:** agree deliverables, publication dates and cross-promotion in writing before recording; decide who publishes what and where; get consent for cross-platform reuse at recording time (`repurposing-guide.md` §12); and expect the subscriber gain to be modest and the relationship to be the durable asset.

## 8. Mining comments for ideas

The channel's own comment section is the best-targeted audience research available, and it is free. Do a pass monthly.

| Comment pattern | What it is telling you | Action |
|---|---|---|
| The same question repeatedly | A video-sized gap you have not filled | Make it — the demand is proven |
| "But what about X?" | An angle the audience wants covered | A follow-up, or a segment in the next video |
| "This didn't work for me because…" | An edge case worth a dedicated video | Troubleshooting video; high search value |
| Disagreement with a claim | A debate worth hosting | Response video or a stronger treatment |
| "Can you do a longer version?" | Format demand | Adjust length for this pillar |
| Confusion at a specific timestamp | A clarity problem | Fix in the next video; check the retention curve there |
| Timestamps posted by viewers | The moments that mattered | Shorts candidates (`repurposing-guide.md` §6) |
| A shared underlying problem | Product demand | The §10 gate in `monetization-guide.md` |

**Method:** sort by newest and by top, sample 50–100 comments across the last several videos, tally repeated questions rather than reading impressionistically, and keep a running idea list in the workspace. Include comments on *competitors'* videos — the unanswered questions there are the clearest content gaps available, and nobody owns them yet.

## 9. Viral moments and pile-ons

**A viral video.** Most channels handle this badly by panicking in one of two directions: chasing it or ignoring it.

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Check where the traffic came from before concluding anything | Assume a repeatable formula from n=1 |
| Ensure the channel's other videos are worth landing on | Rush out a low-quality sequel to catch the wave |
| Add the viral video to a playlist that leads somewhere | Change the channel's whole positioning |
| Pin a comment pointing to the natural next watch | Spam a subscribe request into every reply |
| Expect returning-viewer share to *fall* — that is arithmetic, not decline | Read the post-spike drop as failure |
| Raise sponsorship quotes only once the new median settles (`monetization-guide.md` §5) | Quote against the spike |

A viral video mostly converts to a durable audience if the *next* few videos are good. The window is short; the correct response is to publish the best normal video the creator can, not a cash-in.

**A negative pile-on.** Sequence: (1) stop posting and wait a few hours — the first instinct is almost always wrong; (2) determine honestly whether the criticism is correct; (3) if it is, correct it plainly, once, without defensiveness or self-flagellation; (4) if it is not, usually say nothing — most pile-ons decay faster without fuel; (5) restrict or hold comments while it burns (§4); (6) do not delete the video unless it is actually harmful — deletion reads as concealment and creates a second story; (7) do not respond to every account; (8) return to normal publishing sooner than feels comfortable, because normal output is what ends it.

Never respond in the moment, never respond at night, and never let anyone else post on the channel's behalf while the creator is upset.

## 10. Boundaries and sustainability

This section is short and honest because the failure it describes ends more channels than the algorithm does.

**Parasocial reality.** A one-sided relationship forms whether or not the creator wants it: the audience feels they know a person who does not know them. That attachment is what makes memberships, tips and community work (`monetization-guide.md` §1), and it is also what makes hostility feel personal and demands feel legitimate. Both come from the same source; a creator cannot have one without the other.

**Boundaries that hold, set in advance rather than under pressure:** decide what parts of life are on the channel and keep the rest off permanently — a boundary loosened once cannot be re-tightened. Do not share family members, home location, or real-time whereabouts. Keep a separate business email. Do not treat one-to-one contact as a scalable perk; cap it, price it, and cap the seats (`monetization-guide.md` §8). Set comment hours rather than reading them continuously.

**Pace.** Cadence correlates with growth (`benchmarks.md` §4), and that fact quietly pushes creators into schedules they cannot hold. The resolution is honest: choose a cadence sustainable in a bad month — an illness, a move, a hard week — not the cadence achievable in the best week ever recorded. A break costs weeks of momentum (§4), but burnout costs the channel. Batching production, keeping a two-video buffer, and publishing extras rather than promising extras are the three practical defences. Announce breaks in advance; audiences accept a stated break and punish silent disappearance.

**Warning signs to name out loud when they appear:** dreading the upload; reading comments compulsively; measuring self-worth in views; the buffer at zero for weeks; posting only because of the schedule with nothing to say. These are early signals, not failures — the response is to reduce cadence deliberately, not to push through until something breaks.

## 11. Decision rules

- **If** a channel posts nothing between uploads → start with a publish-day community post and one poll a week. That is the entire starting programme.
- **If** running a poll → make the options concrete video titles, and only run it if every option is one you would actually make.
- **If** a poll and search demand disagree → search demand wins for discovery; the poll wins for the core audience's satisfaction.
- **If** a video just published → treat the first two hours as a work block: pin the seeded comment, reply to everything, heart generously.
- **If** the script has no specific comment question → the comment section will be empty. Add one, and match the pinned comment to it.
- **If** a comment is criticism → engage. **If** trolling → hide, never argue. **If** harassment → block, report, document, do not engage.
- **If** moderation cannot keep up → restrict before disabling; disabling is legitimate, not a defeat.
- **If** asked which metric shows real growth → returning-viewer share, not subscribers.
- **If** the creator wants to announce a schedule → the cadence they can hold in a bad month, never the best-week cadence.
- **If** considering live → require an existing reliable audience; otherwise run announced Q&As instead.
- **If** running a premiere → be present in chat for the entire runtime, or do not premiere.
- **If** converting a stream to VOD → trim, re-thumbnail, re-title, chapter it, extract Shorts. Never leave a raw stream as the upload.
- **If** evaluating a collaboration → adjacent audiences, similar size, one shared premise worth watching. A shoutout swap is not a collaboration.
- **If** looking for video ideas → mine your own comments and your competitors' comments before anything else.
- **If** a video goes viral → publish the best normal video next, not a sequel; do not re-quote sponsorship rates against the spike.
- **If** a pile-on starts → wait, assess honestly, correct once if wrong, otherwise stay quiet, restrict comments, keep publishing.
- **If** a community, live or premiere benchmark is requested → `benchmarks.md` §11 lists these as gaps. Say the benchmark is unavailable.
